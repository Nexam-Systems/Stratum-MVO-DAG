Three Daggers.
One ring. One operator.

STRATUM coordinates up to three Dagger air vehicles onto a standoff ring around a single target you designate — planned on the ground, flown by the aircraft, supervised from one screen.

You designate STRATUM plans The aircraft fly it
The concept

A surveillance ring, built by geometry

You mark one point on the map. STRATUM draws a circle around it — the standoff ring — and places up to three hold stations on that circle. Each Dagger flies to its own station and turns to face the target, giving you three simultaneous eyes-on angles from a safe distance.

The division of labour is deliberate. STRATUM sends intent; the autopilot owns the geometry. You transmit a target and a handful of numbers; each aircraft computes its own hold point, hold altitude and facing heading. There is no link between the aircraft and no need for one — coordination happens on the ground, before the vehicles ever separate.

Because the aircraft do the flying, STRATUM does not need a trajectory generator. It needs a scheduler and a constraint. That single insight is what keeps the whole capability small, proven and safe.

TargetThe single point you designate. One per mission.
Standoff ringA circle of radius R around the target, at height H. Drawn, never guessed.
Ring slotA hold station on the ring, set by its bearing θ from the target. One aircraft per slot.
Transit levelThe altitude an aircraft flies its run-in at — distinct from its hold height.

What STRATUM decides for you

At n=3 the assignment problem has only six possibilities, so STRATUM simply checks them all. It proposes the pairing that gives the shortest total run-in with no crossing paths — the same choice that happens to buy you deconfliction. You can override any slot; if your override would cross two paths, STRATUM flags it with an advisory. It does not block you.

Then, per aircraft

Each Dagger climbs to its own transit level, is committed on a staggered schedule, runs in at that level, and drops to the common hold height only once it is over its own slot.

Execution, one aircraft

Take off & climb

Aircraft climbs to its assigned transit level. altitude confirmed

Commit

STRATUM sends target + bearing, switches the aircraft into Standoff. command accepted

Run-in

Aircraft flies its straight approach at the transit level. nav: standoff

On station

Over its slot, it settles to hold height and faces the target. nav: hold

"On station" means three things, not one

STRATUM only declares an aircraft on station when it has switched into Hold and is within the slot's acceptance radius and is at the right altitude. A vehicle can reach Hold by other routes — an operator action, a failsafe — so all three must hold before the light goes green. Belt and braces, at no cost.

Keeping them apart

Deconfliction by construction

STRATUM never commands an aircraft to dodge another. A ground-commanded avoidance loop cannot close fast enough to be safe — by the time a warning crosses the link and a manoeuvre is acked, a 15 m/s aircraft has already flown through the keepout volume. So separation is guaranteed by the plan, before the aircraft leave the ground, in four independent layers.

1

Structural — at slot-placement time

The wizard enforces a minimum angular separation between slots. You physically cannot drag two stations closer than the safe minimum, so terminal separation is settled on the ground and cannot fail in the air.

before flight · in the UI
2

Vertical — during transit

Every aircraft is assigned a distinct transit level, at least 10 m apart. The run-ins are separated top-to-bottom by construction. This is the workhorse layer, and it costs one extra climb command per aircraft.

before commit · one climb each
3

Assignment & schedule

Crossing-free slot assignment removes lateral path crossings; staggered commit separates the departures in time. Both are computed in the planner before anyone flies.

in the plan · free
4

The monitor — advisory only

In flight, STRATUM watches predicted separation and inflates the safe distance by each aircraft's own GPS accuracy. On a predicted breach it raises an advisory and offers one action — HOLD ALL. It recommends; it never manoeuvres on its own.

in flight · operator-authorised

The bubble is not the criterion

The 1 m body keepout is kept, but the number STRATUM actually enforces grows with measurement uncertainty: with typical GPS accuracy the working minimum is around 7 m, seven times the nominal bubble. A criterion smaller than the measurement noise generates alarms, not safety — so the enforcement lives at slot-placement time, not in a runtime controller.

Terminal transition

From ring to engagement

An orchestrated standoff can transition to a coordinated engagement — the full remit is designate → ring → deconflicted run-in → on station → operator-commanded ensemble engagement, against the same ring target each aircraft already holds. It reuses the existing per-aircraft command path: no new link, no new firmware.

A second authorisation gate

Engagement is a terminal action, so it carries its own explicit authorisation, distinct from and heavier than the ARM gate. ARM clears the flight; this gate clears the terminal transition. The ensemble engage command is inert until you set it.

Abort is operator-only

In the multi-vehicle case there is no self-abort on target loss — an aircraft that loses its target continues its existing single-vehicle behaviour. Abort exists only as an explicit action from your console. STRATUM issues no autonomous abort.

Named risk — operator saturation

No self-abort, several simultaneous terminal actions and one operator is a real workload risk. It is named here, not waved away: it is a first-order item for the flight-test safety case, and abort deconfliction — stratified recovery altitudes, the same discipline as the run-in — is carried into the fault-injection matrix before any live engagement.

Safety posture

The link is not load-bearing

The single most important commitment in this design: because separation is guaranteed by geometry rather than by supervision, losing the link degrades the mission without endangering the aircraft. Ground coordination makes the C2 link mission-critical; deconfliction-by-construction is what keeps it from being safety-critical.

Link lost · one aircraft

It finishes and holds

The aircraft completes its firmware-owned run-in and settles onto its own slot. Safe, because the run-in was deconflicted before commit and never depended on you watching. STRATUM alerts and marks the ensemble degraded.

Link lost · all aircraft

Each completes and holds

Every aircraft finishes and holds on its slot. What happens after the configured timeout is a verified, explicit choice — never the stock default of "nothing".

One aircraft aborts / returns

The others hold as briefed

The ring is not silently re-solved. Re-spacing two survivors is a re-commit under uncertainty — an operator decision. STRATUM offers the re-plan; it does not take it.

Cannot ARM

Failsafe block verified first

A mission cannot arm until STRATUM has read back and verified the required failsafe parameters on every aircraft. A failure blocks the mission and names the offending aircraft and parameter.

One production rule that lives outside software

Each airframe must ship with a unique system ID. Two Daggers with the same ID present as one aircraft — the second silently never appears, a failure that looks like a radio fault and is not one. STRATUM detects and refuses a colliding fleet; the fix belongs in the build and acceptance procedure.

Architectural maturity

Additive, not a rebuild

The capability sits on top of a control chain that is already multi-vehicle everywhere below the operator panel. The orchestration layer iterates its own set of aircraft and calls the same proven per-aircraft commands the single-vehicle path already uses. Nothing that works today regresses.

On the wire, a three-aircraft ring is three single-aircraft standoffs that differ by one number — the bearing. That is the cheapest possible extension of a contract that is already flying.

New wire contractNone — reuses the existing standoff command
Assignment options at n=36 — checked exhaustively, no solver

The remaining risk is not in the flight geometry. It is in the failsafe defaults — where three aircraft would otherwise make independent, uncoordinated decisions at exactly the moment the ground station stops watching.
